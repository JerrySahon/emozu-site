=== en
A meltdown is not a tantrum. The UK's National Autistic Society describes it as an intense response to an overwhelming situation, when a person temporarily loses control of their behaviour. The child may drop to the floor, scream, and not hear you. Then, often quite fast, it passes.

Parents who live with this learn a hard skill: reading the build-up. This article is about that build-up, on two time scales.

## Hours before: the day adds up

A parent who knows their child well can often name what came before the last meltdown:

- a short night or a missed nap;
- a skipped meal, or no water for a while;
- a loud place, a crowd, a busy party;
- a change in routine;
- a day with a lot of emotions, even good ones.

None of these alone makes a meltdown. Together they lower the threshold, and a small thing tips it over. That is why so many parents keep looking for triggers. When we tagged 409 public posts by parents, 47% were about exactly that: trying to find what sets it off.

A simple log works better than memory. See our guide on [keeping a trigger diary](@HOME@blog/meltdown-trigger-diary/).

## Minutes before: the body changes first

Before the behaviour changes, the body does. Researchers have measured this with wearable sensors:

- [Goodwin et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/aur.2151) predicted episodes 1 minute ahead in 20 young people on an inpatient unit.
- [Imbiriba et al., 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/article-abstract/2813185) predicted 3 minutes ahead, AUROC 0.80, in 70 young people across 4 psychiatric hospitals.
- [Kartha et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.17618), a preprint, reached 10 minutes ahead in a special-ed classroom, with the sensor on the ankle.

The signals were heart rate, skin conductance (a sweat response the body makes under stress) and movement. In these studies an "episode" means aggression or other challenging behaviour, which is not always what a parent calls a meltdown.

You can't see skin conductance. But you can often see what the National Autistic Society calls the rumble stage: pacing, asking the same question again and again, rocking, or becoming very still.

## What helps in that window

At the rumble stage there may still be a chance to prevent a meltdown. What helps:

1. **Cut the input.** Move to a quiet, familiar place. Dim the lights, turn off music, ask people to step away.
2. **Check the basics.** Food, water, the toilet, rest. A thirsty child who can't say it may only be able to show it.
3. **Say less.** Short words, a low voice, no questions that need an answer.
4. **Offer what usually calms.** A fidget, music, a soft corner. Touch only if your child finds it calming: some children settle in a firm hug, others need space.
5. **Stay calm yourself.** Your state is part of the room.

If it has already started, give it time. Recovery from overload can take longer than you expect.

## The honest limit

All of the studies above were done in hospitals, clinics or classrooms. Of the 99 children in them, none were followed at home, where a parent needs the warning most. And in the one study that counts alerts, about 2 of 3 were false.

So today a parent's own eye is still the best warning system. [Emozu](@HOME@) is testing whether a soft band can add a few minutes to it, at home. The full evidence is on our [research page](@HOME@research/).
