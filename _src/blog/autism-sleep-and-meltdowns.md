=== en
"He skipped his nap" is often one of the first things a parent says after a hard afternoon. Research supports the link between sleep and difficult days, and the numbers are large.

## How common sleep problems are

- **50 to 80%** of autistic children have sleep problems by parent report, compared with about 25% of other children ([Bellato, Parlatini & Cortese, 2024](https://doi.org/10.20517/rdodj.2023.36)).
- When sleep is measured objectively, autistic children slept about **33 minutes less** per night on average, in a meta-analysis of ten studies ([review, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9868385)).
- Night waking was reported for about **half** of children in one large autism network sample ([ATN/AIR-P Sleep Committee](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11300650)).

The common problems: hard to fall asleep, resisting bedtime, waking at night, and short sleep overall.

## Why it matters for the day

Sleep problems in autistic children are linked with more hyperactive and disruptive behaviour and other daytime problems ([ATN/AIR-P Sleep Committee](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11300650)). This is a link, not proof of cause, but it matches what parents see: after a short night the threshold is lower. The same noise that was fine on Monday can tip things over on Tuesday.

That is why sleep belongs in any log of meltdowns. Without it, a bad afternoon looks random. With it, the pattern often becomes visible.

## What to track

Every day, in the same place as your [trigger diary](@HOME@blog/meltdown-trigger-diary/):

- bedtime and the time your child fell asleep;
- night wakings, roughly how many and how long;
- wake-up time;
- naps: when and how long, or "no nap";
- the next day: episodes, near-misses, general mood.

After a few weeks, compare days after a good night with days after a bad one. Many watches and bands already track sleep well, so a wearable can take this part off your hands. In the Emozu pilot, night data is part of what we record next to the day.

## When to get help

If sleep problems go on, talk to your paediatrician. There are behavioural and medical options, and the right choice depends on the child ([Bellato, Parlatini & Cortese, 2024](https://doi.org/10.20517/rdodj.2023.36)).

Sleep is the slow layer: it sets the threshold for the whole day. The fast layer is the minutes before a meltdown, when the body changes first. Read about that in [Can you see a meltdown coming?](@HOME@blog/meltdown-warning-signs/)

*This article is general information, not medical advice.*
