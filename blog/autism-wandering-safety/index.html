When a child runs: autism, wandering and what actually helps

About half of autistic children try to bolt from a safe place. Why it happens, the layers of protection families use, and where GPS trackers fit.

Yury S., founder of Emozu · November 10, 2026 · 2 min read

Many parents of autistic children know the moment: you look away for a few seconds and the child is gone. Clinicians call it wandering or elopement. It is common, and it can be dangerous.

How common it is

In a study of 1,218 children, 49% had tried to run away from a safe place after age 4. Among those who went missing, 65% had a close call with traffic and 24% with drowning (Anderson et al., Pediatrics, 2012).

Water is the biggest risk. Most deaths after wandering are drownings, around 90% according to Children's National Hospital.

Why children run

Wandering is often a way to communicate. The National Autism Association describes two directions: moving toward something the child loves, like water or trains, or moving away from noise, crowds, fears or demands (summary).

The second kind often follows overload: the same build-up that leads to a meltdown. Knowing your child's triggers helps here too. See how to keep a trigger diary.

Layers that help

No single tool is enough. Families stack several:

  1. A written plan. Autism Speaks has free forms: a family wandering emergency plan, a letter for neighbours, a letter for school (Autism Speaks).
  2. Doors and windows. Alarms, extra locks, and simple visuals like a stop sign on the door (Children's National).
  3. ID. A bracelet or tag with your phone number.
  4. Swimming and water safety lessons. Because water is the main danger.
  5. A tracker. GPS devices show where a child is and can alert you when they leave a set zone. Some US programmes help pay for them: for example, Maryland's Autism Waiver covers GPS devices (Pathfinders for Autism).
  6. Behavioural support. A therapist can work on what your child runs toward or away from.

Where Emozu fits

Emozu does not track location. Good GPS trackers already do that job. What we study is the build-up before overload. If a body signal can warn a parent minutes before, the parent may get time to step in before a child runs from it. Today that is a research question, not a feature. More on our research page.
